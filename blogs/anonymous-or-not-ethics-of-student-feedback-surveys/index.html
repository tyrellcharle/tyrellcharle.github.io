Anonymous or Not? The Ethics of Student Feedback Surveys

Tyrell Charlemagne | Oct 22, 2026 min read

Introduction

Student feedback helps colleges understand what is working and what needs improvement. It can help management decide which courses need to be updated. However, just like with great power comes ethical responsibilities.

In this situation, students must log in with their college username and password to complete a survey that claims to be anonymous. The survey then asks students to rate their instructor from 1-10, so management can use the results to decide which courses need improvement.

I think the college has a valid reason for collecting feedback, but there are many concerns about privacy that must be accounted for, trust, and fairness. Students might not feel comfortable being honest if they think their answers could be traced back to them. Instructors also deserve fair treatment and should not be judged based on one survey. The goal should be to improve courses, not blame people without understanding the full situation.

1. Ethical Issues With the Survey

My first concern is the anonymity. If students have to log in with their college accounts, how do they know their answers cannot be connected to them? Logging in does not automatically mean a survey is not anonymous, but the college needs to explain how the system works and make sure student identities are kept separate from their answers.

This relates to privacy and transparency. Students should know how their information is collected, who can access it, and how it will be used. The college should not promise anonymity unless it has checked that the system can protect it.

Another concern is the power dynamic between students and instructors. Instructors teach students and may grade their work. If students are still attending class or actively waiting for their final grades, they might worry about being penalized for giving a low rating. Even if the college has no intention of doing this, students may still feel uncomfortable sharing their real opinions.

This could affect the results because students might give higher ratings than they believe an instructor deserves just to avoid possible problems.

The rating question also has limitations. Rating an instructor from 1-10 is subjective because students can have different opinions or personal vendetta’s about the same person. A student might give a low rating because they dislike the instructor, while another might struggle because the course is difficult.

Sometimes the problem is not the instructor but the course materials, workload, or schedule. Management needs to separate these issues before deciding what needs to change. I do not think one number is enough to judge an instructor. Ratings can be useful, but they should be considered alongside written comments and other evidence. This relates to fairness and accuracy.

2. Other Ethical Issues

Removing names from survey responses does not guarantee that students cannot be identified. For example, someone might mention a specific incident or conversation in a comment. In a small class, other people might recognize who wrote it. This is called re-identification, when details can reveal someone’s identity even without their name.

Another issue is informed consent. Students should understand why the survey is being conducted, how their answers will be used, and who can see them. They should also know whether participation is voluntary or mandatory.

There is also a risk of data misuse. Management wants to improve courses, which is understandable, but it could make unfair decisions if it treats low ratings as absolutes that an instructor is doing a bad job. The results should instead be investigated instead of being used to make quick a quick judgment under haste.

Data security matters too. The college should limit access to survey responses, protect them from unauthorized access, and decide how long the information needs to be stored.

Finally, the results may not represent every student’s experience. Some students might complete the survey while others skip it because they are busy or do not trust the survey,school,management or educator. Management should remember that the responses may not tell the whole story.

3. My Recommendations to Management

My first recommendation would be to separate login verification from survey answers. The college could check that students are eligible to participate without connecting their identities to their responses.

Second, the college should clearly explain how the survey works. It should be able to tell students whether their answers are anonymous or confidential, who can access the data, and how the information will be used. Anonymous responses cannot reasonably be linked to individuals, while confidential responses may be identifiable but access is restricted.

Third, instructors should not be able to see identifiable student feedback while they are actively teaching or grading those students. Results should be reported in groups, and comments that could reveal someone’s identity should be handled carefully.

I would also recommend making participation voluntary and explaining whether choosing not to participate has any consequences. Students should not feel pressured manipulated or coerced to complete the survey or give positive feedback.

The college should improve its questions by asking separately about teaching, course materials, workload, and course design. This would help management understand what actually needs improvement instead of relying on limited information.

Numerical ratings should be considered alongside written comments and other evidence before important decisions are made. The college should also protect the data, limit access, and delete it when it is no longer needed.

Finally, management should tell students what changes were made because of their feedback. This would show that their opinions matter and help build trust between students and management.

4. What If Management Ignores My Concerns?

If management rejected my recommendations, I would explain the risks again and give clear reasons for my concerns. I would point out that students may not trust the survey if they have to log in but do not know how their identities are protected.

I would document my concerns and management’s response so there was a record of what happened. I would also suggest other ways to collect useful feedback while protecting students.

If management continued to ignore the problem, I would raise my concerns through the appropriate privacy, ethics, or management channels. I would consider the possible harm to students and instructors rather than focusing only on what management wanted.

As a data analyst, I believe I have a responsibility to be honest about the information I work with. I should not claim that a survey is anonymous if this has not been verified, or present results as more reliable than they really are.

If serious concerns remained unresolved, I would have to consider whether I could ethically continue being responsible for the work. Management may make the final decisions, but that does not mean a data analyst should ignore ethical problems. Accountability means taking responsibility for how data is collected, interpreted, and used.

Conclusion

Student surveys can help colleges improve education, but they need to be designed and used responsibly. In this situation, the main concerns are anonymity, student privacy, the power dynamic between students and instructors, and the risk of making unfair decisions based on ratings.

I think the college should explain how it collects and uses information, protect students’ identities, and look at more than one number when evaluating a course or instructor. Students need to trust the process if the college expects honest feedback, and instructors deserve a fair chance to be evaluated properly.

The biggest lesson for me is that data affects real people. Collecting information is not enough. We also need to think about how it was collected, what it actually tells us, and how it could affect others. The goal should be to use feedback to improve courses while protecting students and treating instructors fairly.
